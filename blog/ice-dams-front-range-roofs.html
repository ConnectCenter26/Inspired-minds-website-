Ice Dams on Front Range Roofs: What Causes Them and How to Stop Them

Every winter there's a stretch where Front Range roofs grow a row of icicles along the gutters, and homeowners take it as a sign of a hard winter. Sometimes it is. More often, those icicles are the visible end of an ice dam — a ridge of ice at the roof edge that holds back meltwater until it finds a way under the shingles and into the ceiling. The damage usually shows up weeks later as a brown stain on drywall near an exterior wall, long after anyone remembers the icicles.

The good news: ice dams are predictable, and most of what prevents them can be checked in the fall.

Why they form: it's a heat problem, not a snow problem

An ice dam needs three things at once: snow on the roof, a roof deck warm enough to melt the underside of that snow, and a roof edge cold enough to refreeze the meltwater. The roof deck over a heated attic stays warm; the overhang at the eave sits outside the house and stays at air temperature. Water melts in the middle, runs down, hits the cold eave, and freezes. Repeat for a few days and the ridge of ice gets tall enough to pond water behind it.

The source of that warmth is almost always the house itself — heat leaking into the attic through gaps around recessed lights, bath fans, attic hatches, plumbing and wiring penetrations, and thin or displaced insulation.

Why the Front Range gets them even without big snowpack

Homeowners here sometimes assume ice dams are a Midwest or mountain-town issue. The pattern that drives them is actually common along the Front Range: a snowfall followed by bright, sunny days and below-freezing nights. Strong sun at altitude melts snow on south- and west-facing slopes during the day; everything refreezes after sundown, especially on shaded eaves, north-facing slopes, and valleys where two roof planes meet. You don't need three feet of snow — a few inches that melt and refreeze over and over is enough.

Signs you've had one (even if you never saw it)

  • Stains or peeling paint on ceilings or upper walls along exterior walls, especially below eaves and roof valleys.
  • Heavy icicles in the same spot every winter, while the rest of the roofline stays clear.
  • Patchy melt on the roof after a snow — bare stripes over rafters or bare spots above specific rooms point to heat escaping in those areas.
  • Damaged or pulled-away gutters, bent by the weight of ice rather than by debris.
  • Frost, damp insulation, or dark staining on the attic roof sheathing — visible with a flashlight from the attic hatch.

What actually fixes it — and what just treats the symptom

The real fix is keeping the attic cold. That means air-sealing the ceiling plane (the gaps listed above), bringing insulation up to a consistent depth with no bare spots near the eaves, and making sure soffit and roof vents actually move air — baffles at the eaves keep insulation from blocking the soffit vents. When the whole roof deck stays close to outdoor temperature, snow melts evenly from the sun rather than from below, and there's no warm-middle, cold-edge pattern to build a dam.

The backup layer is at the eaves. A self-adhering ice-and-water membrane under the first few feet of shingles at the eaves and in valleys is what keeps a dam from turning into a leak if one does form. It's installed at re-roof time, so if your roof is due for replacement anyway, this is the moment to confirm it's in the scope — not an upgrade to skip.

Symptom treatments have their place, but know what they are. Heat cables along the eaves can keep a channel open on a problem section, and clearing the bottom few feet of snow with a roof rake from the ground helps after a big storm. Neither changes why the dam forms. Avoid chipping ice with a hammer or climbing an icy roof — both cause more damage (to the roof and to people) than the dam itself.

How this plays out on an actual job

An ice-dam call starts in the attic, not on the roof. The inspection looks for bypasses around fixtures and hatches, measures insulation depth, confirms the soffit vents are open, and notes any staining on the sheathing. That turns into an estimate split into what matters: air sealing and insulation first, ventilation corrections second, and any drywall or roofing repairs from past leaks last — so you're not paying to repaint a ceiling that will stain again next February. The contractor match depends on the scope (insulation and air-sealing crews are a different trade than roofers), and the audit afterward checks that the sealing and insulation actually went where the scope said, before it's buried under fresh insulation and out of sight for good.
