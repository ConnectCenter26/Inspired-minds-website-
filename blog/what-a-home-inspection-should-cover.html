What a Real Home Inspection Should Cover (and What It Won't)

Most homeowners only see an inspection report twice: when they buy the house, and when they sell it. Both times it shows up as a 60-page PDF full of photos and the phrase "recommend evaluation by a qualified professional," and both times it gets skimmed for the scary items and filed away. That's a waste. A good inspection is the cheapest way to find out what your house actually needs, and it's worth knowing what a thorough one looks like before you rely on it.

What a thorough inspection actually covers

A general home inspection is a visual, non-invasive look at the house's major systems as they are on that day. At minimum, expect it to work through:

  • Roof and attic — covering, flashing at chimneys and walls, gutters and downspouts, and from inside the attic: sheathing condition, staining, insulation depth, ventilation, and anything venting into the attic that shouldn't be (like a bath fan).
  • Exterior and grading — siding, trim, windows and doors, decks and stairs, and whether the ground slopes away from the foundation or toward it.
  • Structure — visible foundation walls, cracks and their pattern, signs of movement like sticking doors or sloped floors, and framing where it's exposed in basements and crawlspaces.
  • Plumbing — supply and drain materials, water heater age and venting, visible leaks, water pressure, and fixtures run long enough to see whether drains keep up.
  • Electrical — the panel (opened, not just looked at), wiring type, grounding, GFCI and AFCI protection where expected, and a sampling of outlets and switches.
  • Heating and cooling — operation, age, venting, filter and ductwork condition where visible, and whether every room actually gets air.
  • Interior — walls and ceilings (stains are clues), floors, stairs and railings, and smoke and CO alarms.

Where a standard inspection stops

An inspector doesn't open walls, move the seller's furniture, or climb a roof that isn't safe to walk. Some things are outside a general inspection by design and need a specialist if there's reason to look: sewer lines (that's a camera scope), radon testing, mold sampling, a structural engineer's opinion on foundation movement, and a chimney sweep's look inside the flue. A good report says plainly what wasn't accessible — "attic viewed from hatch only," "crawlspace not entered" — so you know where the blind spots are. If a report doesn't list any limitations, that's not a sign nothing was missed.

Signs the report was rushed

  • Every item ends in "recommend further evaluation" with no description of what was actually observed.
  • No photos of the electrical panel interior, attic, or water heater label — the three places that tell you the most.
  • Generic boilerplate that could describe any house — no measurements, locations, or room names.
  • No sense of priority. A loose cabinet hinge and a reversed-polarity outlet listed at the same weight.

How to read the report so it's actually useful

The single most useful thing you can do with an inspection report is sort it. Put every finding in one of three buckets: safety (electrical hazards, gas or venting issues, missing alarms, unsafe stairs or rails), water and structure (anything letting water in or showing movement — these get more expensive the longer they wait), and maintenance and cosmetic (caulking, worn finishes, aging but working equipment). Then note which trade handles each one. A dozen findings usually collapse into three or four contractor visits, and that's when the report turns into a plan instead of a worry list.

It's also worth getting an inspection when you're not buying or selling. An existing-home inspection before a remodel, after a hailstorm, or when an older furnace or water heater is approaching the end of its life gives you the same system-by-system picture without the closing-date pressure.

How this plays out on an actual job

Our inspections are written to feed the next step, not just to document problems. Each finding gets a location, a photo, a priority, and the trade that fixes it. That becomes a repair estimate grouped by trade and urgency, so you can fix safety and water issues now and schedule the rest. If you want help getting it done, we match you with contractors suited to that specific scope, then audit the finished work against the original finding — so the item actually comes off the list instead of just being painted over.
