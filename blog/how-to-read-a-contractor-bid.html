How to Read a Contractor Bid Before You Sign It

A bid that shows up as one lump-sum number, with no breakdown of what that number actually covers, isn't really a bid. It's a guess dressed up as a quote. The problem is that most homeowners don't find that out until the job is underway and the contractor says, "that wasn't included."

The fix isn't learning to spot a "good" contractor by gut feel. It's knowing what a real scope of work looks like, so you can tell the difference between a bid you can hold someone to and one that's just a number on a page.

What "scope of work" actually means

A proper scope of work breaks a job into individual line items, and each line item carries four things: a specific location, a specific defect or task, a priority, and either a price or an honest flag that it needs more diagnosis before it can be priced. "Fix the bathroom" is not a line item. "Re-grout and caulk shower tile, patch two holes from a removed shelf bracket — bathroom, main level" is a line item.

The priority tier matters as much as the price. On any well-run scope, items get sorted into something like: must-fix (a safety, code, or active-water issue — the kind that gets worse if you wait), nice-to-have (cosmetic, no urgency), and monitor (a symptom that's real but not yet diagnosed enough to price responsibly — a musty smell from a washer, a stain that might be old or might be active). A contractor who prices everything the same, with no distinction between "this could hurt someone" and "this would look nicer," hasn't actually assessed your house — they've just walked through it.

Why "monitor" and "needs further inspection" are good signs, not red flags

It feels counterintuitive, but a bid that says "can't price this until we open the wall" on one line item is more trustworthy than a bid that confidently prices everything on the first walkthrough. Some things genuinely can't be scoped from a visual inspection — a water stain at the base of a door might be old and resolved, or it might be an active leak two rooms away working its way downhill. A contractor who's willing to say "I don't know yet" on that line, instead of guessing a number to keep the total looking clean, is the one being straight with you.

Red flags in a bid

  • One number, no line items. If you can't tell what you're paying for room by room, you can't tell what's missing.
  • No priority distinction. Everything treated as equally urgent (or equally optional) usually means no real assessment happened.
  • Fixed prices on unselected materials. If you haven't picked a fixture, tile, or finish yet, the "price" for that item should be a range or an allowance — not a flat number that locks you in before you've chosen anything.
  • No payment schedule. A bid without milestones tied to completed work is a bid that assumes you'll pay on trust alone.
  • No mechanism for surprises. Once a wall or floor is open, something unexpected turns up more often than not. A bid that doesn't say how that gets handled is a bid that becomes an argument later.

Questions worth asking before you sign anything

Ask the contractor to break the total into line items if it isn't already. Ask which items are firm prices versus estimates pending further inspection, and get that distinction in writing. Ask what happens, procedurally, if they find something once work starts — not "will you charge me more," but what the actual process is for pricing and approving it. A contractor who answers these clearly, without getting defensive, is telling you something useful about how the rest of the job will go.

Where this fits into the bigger picture

This is the same discipline behind a proper inspection: identify the actual condition, sort it by real urgency, and only price what can honestly be priced — flagging the rest instead of guessing. A bid built on a vague or incomplete scope isn't just a communication problem; it's usually a sign the underlying inspection was rushed too.
